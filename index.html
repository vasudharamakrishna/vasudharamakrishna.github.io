The Cost of Service: The Short- and Long-Term Effects of WWII Injuries

With Michael Briskin and Yicheng Chen

Abstract

Limited data on wartime experience makes it hard to estimate the costs of battle injuries on veterans’ outcomes. We construct a new dataset of U.S. Army hospital admissions during World War II linked to the census and death records, to estimate these costs. We find that battle-injured veterans were less likely to be employed or gain education after the war. Those with more severe injuries or mental illness saw greater labour market penalties. Battle-injured men were more likely to get married but to women who were less educated and less likely to work. They experienced a quarter of a year loss in life, and lived in poorer neighbourhoods at death.

WEOrg

From 2023 to 2025, I was a co-organizer of BU Women & Nonbinary People in Economics (WEOrg). We wanted to create a space that brings together marginalized people of different backgrounds in the department and to foster a more diverse and equitable environment.

In 2025 we organized the second edition of WERISE, which brought together scholars to discuss intersectional representation in the economics profession and to highlight research on gender in labor, development and health.
